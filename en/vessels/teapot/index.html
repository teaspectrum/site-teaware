Teapot

Vessel

A brewing vessel with a spout, handle and lid. Materials and shapes vary widely, and heat retention is usually better than a gaiwan. Pouring, capacity and comfort matter more than appearance.

Structure and use

A teapot has a body, lid, spout, handle and filter holes. The spout and filter determine flow and whether leaf blocks the pour; the lid affects heat retention and safety; the handle affects balance. A teapot suits brewing that needs heat retention, longer steeping or several cups at once, while a gaiwan is often better for fast decanting.

Materials

Zisha, porcelain, ceramic, glass and metal are all used. Zisha holds heat and may retain aroma, making it suitable for a fixed tea type; porcelain is neutral and easy to clean, useful for comparing teas; glass shows colour and leaf; metal needs attention to material, coating and heat source. Domestic porcelain and zisha are covered by GB/T 3532-2022 and GB/T 10816-2024 respectively.

Shape and pour

Body shape affects leaf expansion and heat retention. Spout length, diameter and filter size affect flow. A round pot holds heat but is harder to clean; a flat pot cools faster; a straight spout pours cleanly. A filter that is too fine clogs easily; one that is too coarse lets fragments through. Test the pot with water before buying: watch the pour, the cut-off and the lid.

How to choose

Check whether the pour is smooth, the cut-off clean, the lid stable, the handle comfortable and the capacity suited to the tea you drink. For one or two people, 100–200 ml is common; larger pots suit groups or boiling. Do not choose only by appearance, maker reputation or clay stories; practical function comes first.

Use and care

Rinse with warm water before first use; avoid long soaking in detergent. Zisha is often kept for one tea type to avoid mixing aromas; porcelain and glass are easier to clean. Empty the leaf, rinse and air-dry after each use. Dry the lid separately to reduce odours.

Shape and tea type

A round belly lets leaf open and suits oolong, black tea and pu-erh; a flat body heats evenly and suits green and white tea; small pots suit gongfu, large pots suit sharing. A short spout with smooth flow makes timing easier; the lid should seat well and not fall when pouring. Zisha holds heat and aroma, porcelain does not distort, glass shows leaf shape.

Flow and cut-off

A good pot pours a clean stream and stops without dripping. Slow flow keeps leaf steeping and increases bitterness; a poor cut-off wets the table. Test with seven-tenths water and three pours, watching flow, lid and spout. The handle should balance the body even when full.

Capacity and sharing

The pot and number of cups set leaf dose and pace. A pot about 1.2 times the total cup volume is handy; too small means constant refilling, too large means leaf sits too long. For groups, decant into a pitcher so every cup has the same strength.

Common misconceptions

  • Is zisha always better than porcelain? No. Material and shape should match the tea and setting.
  • Is a finer spout better? No. Too fine a filter clogs and pours poorly.
  • Should the lid be very tight? No. Too tight can be hard to open; too loose risks dropping.
  • Can one pot brew every tea? Zisha may retain aroma; porcelain and glass are more neutral.

Testing a pot

Fill a new pot with water and pour five times, checking the stream, the cut-off and whether the lid leaks. Then fill it seven-tenths with hot water, lift with one hand and feel the balance and handle heat. Only then brew: compare the same tea, water and time against a gaiwan to see whether aroma is absorbed or the body thinned. Price does not make a pot handy; flow and balance do.

Lid and spout details

The lid should turn slightly without rattling and stay on when pouring; the knob should be tall enough not to burn fingers. The spout interior should be smooth, with a single stream that does not split; too thin a lip chips, too thick cuts poorly. An inner filter should be removable: too fine clogs, too coarse lets dust through.
